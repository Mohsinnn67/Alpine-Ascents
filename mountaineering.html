THE MOUNTAIN FIELD GUIDE

Mountaineering

Mountaineering is the sport of reaching or attempting to reach high points in mountainous regions. Terrain and weather can create serious hazards, so experience, preparation, and sound decisions matter as much as physical ability.

HistoryTypes & stylesTechniquesSheltering

01 / CONTEXT

A brief history of mountaineering

People have crossed mountain landscapes for millennia. The modern sport grew from exploration, local knowledge, scientific travel, and the desire to climb difficult peaks.

1786

Mont Blanc and the rise of alpinism

Jacques Balmat and Michel-Gabriel Paccard reached the summit of Mont Blanc in the Alps. Their ascent helped spark a new era of recreational and exploratory climbing.

1950

First ascent of an 8,000-metre peak

French climbers Maurice Herzog and Louis Lachenal reached Annapurna I. The expedition marked a major milestone in high-altitude mountaineering, achieved at substantial human cost.

1953

Everest summit

Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary reached the summit of Everest on a British-led expedition. Their achievement depended on a large team, including Sherpa climbers and support staff.

TODAY

A wider, more accountable sport

Climbing now spans local crags and remote ranges. Training, community leadership, environmental care, and recognition of local mountain cultures are central to responsible practice.

Context matters

Mountains are homes, sacred places, and working landscapes as well as climbing objectives. Learn local names, customs, access rules, and history before a trip.

02 / DISCIPLINES

Types & styles

The terrain, altitude, season, and objective shape the skills and equipment a climb requires.

01

Hillwalking & scrambling

Routes on trails and rough ground, sometimes involving hands-on movement over easy rock. Navigation and weather judgment matter even when ropes are not used.

Terrain: trails, ridges, easy rock

02

Alpine climbing

Mixed routes through rock, snow, and ice, often completed in a single push. Changing conditions make route planning and efficient transitions essential.

Terrain: mixed mountain routes

03

Expedition climbing

Longer objectives at altitude, usually involving staged camps, acclimatization, load carries, and a larger support plan.

Terrain: remote, high-altitude ranges

04

Rock climbing

Climbers use hands, feet, ropes, and protection on rock faces. Trad, sport, and multi-pitch climbing each involve different equipment and systems.

Terrain: cliffs, walls, and faces

05

Ice & mixed climbing

Steep ice or routes combining rock and ice. Specialized tools, crampons, belaying, and instruction from qualified mentors are required.

Terrain: frozen waterfalls and alpine faces

06

Solo & fast-and-light

These are styles, not beginner shortcuts. Reduced team size or equipment can sharply increase consequences and demands expert judgment.

Approach: advanced, objective-specific

Choose the right objective

Match the route to the least experienced person in the team, current conditions, and available training. A lower objective can be the best climb of the day.

03 / PRACTICE

Techniques & systems

Good technique is practiced before it is needed. Take hands-on instruction from qualified guides for rope, glacier, snow, and ice skills.

A

Plan the route

Study maps, guidebooks, access notes, weather, daylight, escape options, and objective hazards. Set a turnaround time and agree on decision points before leaving.

B

Move as a team

Use a pace that supports communication and safe footing. Rope systems and spacing must suit the terrain and be managed by trained climbers.

C

Use equipment correctly

Know how to fit and inspect boots, helmets, harnesses, ropes, crampons, and ice axes. Practice knots, belaying, anchors, and transitions with a qualified instructor.

D

Navigate and communicate

Carry a map and compass and know how to use them; do not rely only on a phone. Keep the team informed about route changes, fatigue, symptoms, and conditions.

04 / OVERNIGHT

Sheltering in the mountains

A shelter protects the team from exposure, but it cannot make an unsafe site or worsening conditions safe. Choose shelter based on forecast, terrain, season, and local rules.

ESTABLISHED

Mountain huts

Staffed or unstaffed huts provide a fixed refuge on some routes. Check opening dates, reservations, water, heating, and emergency access in advance.

PORTABLE

Four-season tents

Designed for wind and snow when properly pitched and anchored. Select a protected site away from avalanche paths, cornices, rockfall, and water channels.

EMERGENCY

Bivouac shelters

A bivouac is an unplanned or minimalist night outdoors. Carry suitable emergency insulation and shelter; do not treat a bivouac as a substitute for a sound itinerary.

SPECIALIST

Snow shelters

Snow caves and trenches require training, stable snow, ventilation, and awareness of collapse and carbon-monoxide risks. Never dig into an avalanche-prone slope.

Pack out all waste, protect water sources, and follow local camping restrictions and Leave No Trace guidance.

Mountaineering is inherently risky. This guide is educational and does not replace professional instruction or route-specific planning.
