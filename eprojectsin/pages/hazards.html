05 / RISK MANAGEMENT

Hazards & decisions

Mountain risk changes with weather, terrain, and the people in your team. Information helps, but no checklist removes risk. Be willing to change plans or turn around.

01

Monitor / retreat

Wind, lightning, whiteout, cold, and heat can become dangerous quickly. Check several forecasts, carry protective layers, and identify safe exits. Turn back or seek shelter if conditions exceed the team's plan.

02

Monitor / retreat

Headache, nausea, dizziness, and unusual fatigue can indicate altitude illness. Acclimatize gradually; worsening symptoms require stopping ascent and prompt descent. Severe confusion, loss of coordination, or breathing difficulty at rest is an emergency.

03

Avoid exposed slopes

Terrain traps, recent snowfall, wind loading, warming, and weak layers increase avalanche danger. Avalanche terrain requires formal training, current local forecasts, and practiced use of beacon, shovel, and probe. Choose conservative routes and avoid exposure when conditions are uncertain.

04

Protect / reroute

Loose ground, steep ice, and hidden glacier cracks can injure or trap climbers. Use terrain-appropriate protection and rope systems under qualified instruction. Helmets reduce some impact risks but cannot make hazardous terrain safe.

05

Pause / reassess

Slow pace, poor fueling, and exhaustion erode judgment. Take breaks, eat and drink regularly, and speak up early when your condition changes. Adjust or abandon the objective before fatigue compromises safe movement.

Emergency plan

Before the trip, share your route and return time with a trusted contact. Know the local emergency number, carry a communication method that works in the area, and understand the limits of your rescue equipment.

This information is educational and is not a substitute for qualified instruction, local forecasts, or emergency services.
